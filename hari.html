A Living Tradition

The Heritage of Khmer Food

For centuries, Khmer cuisine has been shaped by the Mekong River, ancient temples, and a deep connection to the land. Discover the stories behind Cambodia's most beloved dishes.

Iconic Khmer Dishes

Each dish carries centuries of tradition, from the royal kitchens of Angkor to street-side stalls today.

🍛
National Dish

Amok អាម៉ុក

A fragrant coconut curry steamed in banana leaves. Amok is Cambodia's most famous dish, often made with fish, lemongrass, galangal, and kaffir lime. Its name comes from the Khmer word for "to steam," and it reflects the ancient fusion of Indian and Khmer culinary traditions.

🌿 Coconut & Lemongrass 🔥 Steamed in banana leaf
🍜
Breakfast

Kuy Teav គុយទាវ

A clear pork or chicken broth with rice noodles, herbs, and crispy fried garlic. Kuy Teav is the quintessential Cambodian breakfast, believed to have originated from Chinese traders but adapted over centuries into a uniquely Khmer comfort food.

🥣 Rice noodles 🌅 Morning staple
🥩
Street Food

Lok Lak លុកឡាក់

Stir-fried beef served with rice, fresh salad, and a signature black pepper-lime dip. Lok Lak is a legacy of French colonial influence, blending European steak techniques with Khmer flavors. It's a favorite at local eateries and family gatherings.

🥩 French-Khmer fusion 🍚 Served with rice
🍝
Breakfast

Nom Banh Chok នំបញ្ចុក

Known as "Khmer noodles," this dish features fermented rice noodles topped with a fresh fish gravy, banana blossoms, cucumbers, and a medley of herbs. It is deeply tied to Khmer New Year and is traditionally sold by women carrying baskets on a pole across their shoulders.

🌸 Khmer New Year 🐟 Freshwater fish
🐟
Condiment

Prahok ប្រហុក

Often called "Cambodian cheese," Prahok is a fermented fish paste that forms the backbone of Khmer cooking. It is used to season soups, dips, and stir-fries, adding a deep umami flavor. Its production is a seasonal tradition tied to the Tonle Sap's unique flood cycle.

🌊 Tonle Sap tradition 🧂 Fermented umami
🍙
Festival

Num Ansom នំអន្សម

Sticky rice cakes filled with banana or pork, wrapped in banana leaves and boiled for hours. Num Ansom is prepared for Pchum Ben (Ancestors' Day) and other major festivals. The slow cooking process is a communal activity that strengthens family bonds.

🎋 Banana leaf wrapped 🙏 Festival offering

A Story of Resilience & Flavor

01

Ancient Roots

Khmer cuisine dates back to the Funan and Chenla kingdoms, influenced by Indian spices, Thai aromatics, and Chinese techniques.

02

Angkor's Bounty

The Angkor Empire developed sophisticated irrigation systems, allowing rice cultivation and a thriving culinary culture centered on the temple complexes.

03

French Influence

In the 19th and 20th centuries, French colonialism introduced baguettes, pâté, and coffee, which were creatively adapted into Khmer cuisine.

04

Modern Revival

After the Khmer Rouge era, Cambodian chefs and families have worked to preserve and celebrate traditional recipes, passing them to new generations.

"Khmer food is not just about taste — it is memory, identity, and a connection to the ancestors. Every dish tells a story of the land and its people."

— Cambodian Food Heritage Association
